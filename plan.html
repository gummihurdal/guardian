Business plan, draft of 13 October 2026

Evidence as a service.

Guardian sells one thing: monthly, independent evidence that a small company's backups would restore and that its equipment is not exposed to the internet. The customer already owns backup software and a firewall; what they lack is proof, and from 2026 proof is what the law, the insurer and their largest customer demand.

The event that makes the pitch

In the night of 11–12 October 2026, attackers reached Danfoss refrigeration controllers at French supermarkets, among them Intermarché and Netto stores (about 150 according to Les Mousquetaires, 300 or more according to AFP) and sites of Coopérative U and other chains; further stores were disconnected as a precaution and losses are expected in the tens of millions of euros. The vendor said the affected units were older models whose security software could not be updated against a risk it had already identified, and a security firm had flagged the weaknesses shortly before. Similar disruptions had hit US military commissaries in late August. Source: E24, BBC and Reuters reporting, 13 October 2026.

Every lesson in that story is a line in the Guardian report: know what is reachable, know what is unsupported, disconnect what cannot be fixed, and be able to prove you did.

Market

  • Who: companies with 10 to 500 staff in Switzerland, Germany and Austria, and the managed-service providers (MSPs) who run their IT. First verticals: food retail and distribution, pharmacies, clinics, hotels, logistics, property management, where operational equipment sits on the network.
  • How many: there are more than 3.5 million SMEs in Germany alone and roughly 600,000 in Switzerland; Veeam and Proxmox count hundreds of thousands of installations across DACH. If one in a thousand buys Guardian, that is several thousand customers.
  • What they buy today: backup software and an IT provider. Neither gives independent evidence. Compliance consultants give evidence once a year for €5,000 and up. Guardian gives it monthly for €199.
  • Competition: enterprise tools (Veeam ONE, security-rating services such as those used by large insurers) are priced and designed for big companies. The small-company evidence gap is open.

Scope. Every service is non-intrusive: public data and read-only connections. Guardian does not perform penetration tests or exploitation; that keeps liability, insurance and criminal-law exposure (Art. 143bis StGB in Switzerland, § 202a StGB in Germany) on the safe side and keeps every check automated.

Milestones. €1,000 a month is five Company customers or two Providers and one Company. Thirty Company customers and ten Providers is about €125,000 a year at roughly 85% gross margin, run in home-office hours with Claude generating the reports.

Channels, in order of expected return: the founder's professional network and LinkedIn; Swiss and German MSP communities, where one partner multiplies sites; insurance brokers, who want clients to pass underwriting; and content, starting with a plain-language explainer on the French freezer attack in German, French and English.

Built with AI, deliberately. Claude Code builds the collectors and scanners; Claude writes every report from structured findings, in the customer's language; the founder reviews and signs attestations. This is why the price can be a tenth of a consultant's.

What would raise it substantially

  • Two paying pilot customers from the founder's network (+2).
  • One insurance broker or cyber insurer who recommends the report to clients (+1).
  • One MSP partner bringing ten sites on the Provider plan (+1).
  • Written employer clearance for the side business, removing the main personal risk (+1 on capital and time).

The rule that follows: spend under €5,000 and two months to get two pilots live. If both convert to paid, build the rest. If neither does, the evidence says the market wants it but not from a part-time founder, and the loss is small.
